What to explain in the interview

Discuss these in order. Explain why you chose each mechanism, not just its definition.

ConceptWhat to discuss
1. RequirementsCache or persistent database? GET/PUT/DELETE? Value sizes, traffic, latency, durability, and consistency expectations.
2. APIs and scaleDefine request/response behavior. Estimate storage, read/write QPS, and replication overhead.
3. Single-node storageHow writes survive crashes: WAL → memtable → SSTables. Briefly mention indexes, Bloom filters, and compaction.
4. PartitioningHow a key maps to a server: consistent hashing or logical partitions. Discuss rebalancing and hot keys.
5. ReplicationWhere copies live, how requests reach them, and what failures they can survive. Partitioning splits; replication copies.
6. ConsistencyWhen a write succeeds and what a read can return. Choose leader-based consensus or a leaderless approach deliberately. For leaderless quorums, explain N/R/W, and why overlap alone is not linearizability.
7. Concurrent writesHow you detect and resolve conflicting updates: version vectors, last-write-wins, or application-specific merging.
8. Failure and recoveryNode failures, network partitions, uncertain write outcomes, retries, replica repair, and safe ownership changes.
9. Operational trade-offsHotspots, deletion/TTL, monitoring, security, and backups. Cover briefly unless asked to deepen.

The most important demonstration: trace one write, one read, and one failure. Explain what happens on disk, across replicas, and what the client sees.

45 minutes: 5 clarifying + 10 architecture + 20 deep dives + 10 failures and trade-offs. Aim for a coherent design, not every advanced mechanism.

A quick interview conversation

Practice script, not a video transcript. Use this to explain your choices; adapt the assumptions to the interviewer.

Interviewer
Design a key-value store.
You
Should it be a cache or a durable database? Do we need strong consistency? What traffic and value sizes should I plan for?
Interviewer
Durable, distributed, small values. Eventual consistency is acceptable. Choose reasonable scale assumptions.
You
I'll assume 100 million entries averaging 1 KB and 100K requests/second, mostly reads. I'll support GET, PUT, and DELETE, with no cross-key transactions initially.
Interviewer
What is your high-level architecture?
You
A coordinator routes each key using consistent hashing. Each key has three replicas on distinct machines. Locally, I'll use a WAL, memtable, and SSTables.
Interviewer
When do you acknowledge a write? What does a read return?
You
With N=3, I'll require W=2 durable acknowledgments and R=2 read responses. I'll compare versions and handle conflicts. The sets overlap, but that alone does not guarantee linearizability.
Interviewer
What if replicas disagree or a machine fails?
You
Version vectors detect concurrent updates; the application needs a merge policy. Read repair and anti-entropy fix divergence. Hinted handoff can forward temporarily held writes after recovery.
Interviewer
What is the main trade-off? What if I need strong consistency?
You
This design accepts temporary divergence and pays for repair. For linearizable operations, I'd switch to consensus-backed leaders and safe reads, accepting unavailable operations when a majority cannot be reached.

SDE-2 / 45-minute interview

Design a key-value store

Clarify > Draw > Deep dive > Defend

Baseline: persistent, leaderless, Dynamo-style store. Eventual convergence with an explicit conflict policy.

Independent revision aid, not a verified transcript summary. Video. Timings are a guide; follow interviewer prompts.

  • 01 / Clarify0-5 min / agree on the problem

    • Requirements

      • Cache or durable database?
      • GET, PUT, DELETE; small values.
      • Read/write QPS, p99 latency, durability, availability.
      • Eventual or strong consistency?

      Exclude joins, range queries, and cross-key transactions initially.

    • Size it briefly

      100M x 1 KB ~ 100 GB raw.
      Three copies ~ 300 GB + overhead.

      100K QPS, 90% reads:
      90K reads/s + 10K writes/s.

      Include logs, indexes, growth, and failure headroom.

    • API contract

      GET(key) > value + version
      PUT(key, value, context)
      DELETE(key)

      Define NOT_FOUND, validation errors, and unavailable/timeout responses.

      Timeout != failed write. Protect retries with request IDs or version checks where needed.

  • 02 / Draw5-15 min / explain the architecture

    • One-node storage

      WAL recovers writes; memtable holds recent updates; SSTables persist sorted records.

      Reads use indexes, Bloom filters, and cache. Compaction merges files.

      A buffered log append is not necessarily durable: define the fsync policy.

    • Partition the keys

      Consistent hashing limits movement on membership changes. Virtual nodes improve balance.

      Hot keys remain hot. Even key counts do not mean even traffic.

    • Replicate & route

      N = 3 replicas on distinct machines/failure domains. Coordinator gathers responses.

      Partitioning splits; replication copies. Coordinator != permanent leader.

  • 03 / Deep dive15-35 min / focus on 1-2 hard parts

    • Quorums & consistency

      R = read responses.
      W = durable write acknowledgments.

      R + W > N: fixed-set overlap

      N=3, R=2, W=2: each operation tolerates one unavailable replica if the other two respond.

      Overlap != linearizability. Concurrency, ordering, and membership still need a protocol. Sloppy quorums may break overlap.

    • Trace one write + read

      Write: route > version > replicas persist > W acks > reply

      Read: collect R versions > compare > resolve / return siblings > repair

      W acknowledgments do not imply all N replicas have the write.

    • Concurrent writes

      • Last-write-wins: simple; may discard updates; clock skew matters.
      • Version vectors: detect causal order or concurrency.
      • Resolution: application merge, sibling versions, or suitable CRDT.

      Detecting a conflict does not resolve it.

  • 04 / Defend35-45 min / failures & trade-offs

    • Failure & repair

      • Heartbeats + gossip: suspect failure, not proof.
      • Hinted handoff: forward temporarily held writes.
      • Read repair: fix divergence found on reads.
      • Anti-entropy: background repair; Merkle hashes locate differences.

    • Essential follow-ups

      • Delete / TTL: retain tombstones safely; avoid resurrection.
      • Rebalance: snapshot > catch up > safe ownership switch.
      • Monitor: p99 latency, quorum errors, disk, repair lag.
      • Authentication, quotas, backups.

      Replication is not a backup. Expand these only if asked.

    • Close with the trade-off

      During a partition, linearizable consistency and CAP availability cannot both be guaranteed.

      Need strong consistency? Switch explicitly to consensus-backed leaders, majority commits, and safe linearizable reads; no majority can mean unavailable operations.

      "We scale through partitioning and survive failures through replication, at the cost of repair and conflict-handling complexity."

10 quick FAQs

Short answers to common interview follow-ups.

  1. Why choose a key-value store instead of SQL?

    It fits known-key lookups and updates with simple access patterns. If joins, flexible filtering, or relational transactions dominate, a relational database may be the better choice.

  2. Why not keep everything in a hash map?

    A hash map alone does not provide crash recovery, disk storage, replication, or distributed routing. It is a useful component, not a complete durable database.

  3. How do partitioning and replication differ?

    Partitioning splits different keys across machines to scale capacity. Replication copies the same keys onto independent machines to improve resilience.

  4. Does consistent hashing solve hot keys?

    No. It distributes key ownership, not request popularity. Cache hot reads with explicit staleness rules; splitting a hot logical key requires application-aware semantics.

  5. Does R + W > N guarantee strong consistency?

    No. It guarantees read/write set intersection for a fixed replica set. Linearizability also requires appropriate ordering, concurrency handling, and read/write protocols.

  6. What does a timed-out write mean?

    The outcome is unknown: some replicas may already have stored it. Use deduplication or conditional version checks where needed so retries do not introduce stale or duplicate effects.

  7. Do version vectors resolve conflicts?

    No. They detect causal order and concurrent versions. Application merge rules, sibling reconciliation, or a suitable CRDT determine the resulting value.

  8. How are hinted handoff and anti-entropy different?

    Hinted handoff forwards known writes held for an unavailable replica. Anti-entropy compares replica state to discover and repair divergence, including data that is rarely read.

  9. Why use tombstones instead of deleting immediately?

    Older values may remain in files or on stale replicas. A versioned deletion marker suppresses them; removing it too early can resurrect deleted data.

  10. Are three replicas enough to replace backups?

    No. Accidental deletion and bad writes can replicate too. Keep separate backups and test restores; place replicas across independent failure domains.
